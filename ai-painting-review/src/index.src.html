I · The question

What does existing research establish about viewers’ responses to AI-generated digital paintings, and how does that inform two separate questions?

Art status. Do viewers, or a theory of art, accept the image as art?

Artist status. Does that make the system an artist? Accepting the first does not settle the second.

III · My interpretation · Mahmoud Hafez
The image

Can be art

Through human artistic practice and viewer engagement: art status granted readily in scenarios, and selection and editing in practice.

The model as independent artist

Not established

The experiments measure viewers’ ratings and attributions, not a system’s agency. Outcome or relational theories could grant more. An expressive image is not evidence of machine consciousness, nor proof that it is impossible.

III · Why it matters

Three consequences beyond the gallery.

01 · TRUST
Labels shape how human work is judged, too.

Attribution moves perceived depth and worth, and comparison can lift or lower human work.

02 · WHOSE TASTE
Evaluation can privilege some traditions.

Most studies sample one country, and a widely used aesthetic scorer favours realistic landscapes, cities and portraits by Western and Japanese artists.

03 · CREDIT
Opaque workflows misplace credit.

One “AI” label hides prompting, selection and editing, and the network behind a work.

III · Limitations → research questions

What the studies could not show…

…becomes the next question.

Each card carries its limitation across to the question that would address it. These are proposals, not predictions. Gold notes mark questions that 2026 research is already investigating.

Where the field is · my reading, not a formal stage model

Experiments, reviews and a meta-analysis exist. A validated test of artistry across systems and cultures does not.

Forecast · not a measured trend

More studies of the human–AI process and culturally varied reception than of “human vs. AI” labels alone.

The image

Can qualify as art.

The model as artist

Still contested.
