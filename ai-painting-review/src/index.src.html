Some studies instead put an “AI” label on human paintings, or used stories about robots. Those are included as comparisons.

Question 1 · Aesthetic value

Fifty pairs. Two separate groups. Two different questions.

Group 1 was asked

“Which do you prefer?”

Group 2 was asked

“Which one is AI?”

Each pair: a lesser-known painting by a famous artist, and a DALL·E 2 image matched to it by artist and genre. Group 1 preferred the AI image more often than chance; group 2 still spotted it more often than chance.

  • Each dot is one pair. Right = the AI image was preferred. Up = it was spotted.
  • The lines at 50 mark pure guessing.
  • r = .42: the AI images people liked more were also the ones they spotted more easily.
  • Below 50: for some pairs, most people picked the human painting as the AI one.

A “human” label raised every rating, most for worth and profundity.

Longer bars mean the label made a bigger difference (Cohen’s d). 30 Artbreeder paintings, labels at random, 149 adults, Study 1. In Study 2, paintings that seemed to take more effort gained more from a “human” label.

Question 3 · Does art need an artist?
A story, like those participants read

A robot driven by artificial intelligence paints a landscape.

Is the painting art?

Is the robot an artist?

Participants read short stories, no images. The stories varied who painted, whether it was on purpose, and the style.

The painting

Judged art about as readily as a human’s painting, though this varied across story versions.

Also in Horton et al.: 87% of people shown only “AI”-labelled images still called most of them art.

The robot

Much less often called an artist, partly because people saw less artistic intention in it.

My interpretation

The painting

An AI-generated painting can count as art, through the people who make, choose and look at it.

The model

Calling the model an independent artist is a separate claim, and this research does not establish it.

An outcome-focused theory could grant more. And no study shows machine consciousness; an expressive image is not evidence of it.

This drawing was made by code, not a hand. Does that change it for you?

Question 4 · What to study next

Labels shape trust in human work, who gets credit, and whose taste counts. Each limit points to a next question.

Limitation

Studies tested older tools: Artbreeder, DALL·E 2.

Next question

Do the effects hold for today’s models, named and versioned?

Limitation

Labels said only “AI” or “human”, hiding how the work was made.

Next question

Does showing the human workflow change judgments?

Horton: human–AI collaborations rated between the two. Rondini: human guidance raised AI drawings.

Limitation

Mostly quick online ratings of single images.

Next question

What happens in real galleries, and over time?

Chiarella: at a real art fair, the penalty appeared in only one order.

Limitation

Mostly one-country samples, and tools with a narrow taste.

Next question

Do findings hold across cultures and kinds of art?

Oksanen: 37 of 44 studies sampled one country. Taylor: a popular aesthetic scorer favours Western and Japanese art.

Recent work is moving from who supposedly made the image to how human guidance shapes production (Rondini: a controlled drawing task) and whose taste our evaluation tools reward (Taylor: an audit of one aesthetic predictor). Neither settles painting generators in general. These are proposed questions, not predictions.

The painting

Often, yes.

The model as artist

Still an open question.
